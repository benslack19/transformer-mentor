Reading the trend: a big early gain, then stability

📈 Early jump: Win rate rose from 44% → 52% (+8%) between the first and second model snapshot.

📉 Plateau since: The remaining 6 snapshots fluctuate within ±1% — effectively flat.

📊 Trend test result: No statistically significant monotonic upward trend across all 8 batches (p = 0.11). The model is not consistently getting better batch-over-batch.

Should We Launch or Keep Iterating?

Recommendation: Launch with a staged rollout and monitoring plan

Why launch now
  • New model wins 54% of tasks vs 26% — a meaningful margin
  • The difference is statistically robust (not due to random variation)
  • Win rate has plateaued — continuing lab iteration has diminishing returns
  • Production traffic will yield faster, higher-quality signal for the next improvement cycle
Conditions before / after launch
  • Review the specific error types where the new model underperforms (see slide 11)
  • Start with a staged rollout (e.g., 10% of traffic)
  • Monitor win rate weekly — pause if it falls below 50% at 2 standard deviations
  • For future eval rounds: randomly assign graders to tasks rather than self-selection

Appendix

How We Computed Uncertainty: Bootstrap Confidence Intervals

↖ Relates to: Slides 5–11 (all charts with error bars)

ELI5 (plain English)

Imagine you want to know the average rating in a city, but you only measured 1,000 people. What if you'd measured a slightly different 1,000?

Bootstrap answers this by repeatedly re-drawing 1,000 people from your existing data (with replacement) and recomputing the average — 5,000 times. The spread of those 5,000 averages becomes your confidence range.

In plain terms: "If we'd run this study with slightly different data, our estimate would land somewhere in this range 95% of the time."

Technical details

Resample n = 2,699 observations with replacement, 5,000 iterations. 95% CI: 2.5th–97.5th percentile of bootstrap distribution.

No distributional assumptions required — robust to non-normal and ordinal data.

Example: For win rate = 54.0%:
Bootstrap → 95% CI [52.1%, 55.9%].
We are 95% confident the true win rate falls in this range.

Note: wider CIs in early batches (n ≈ 97) vs later batches (n ≈ 969) reflect the smaller sample sizes — not more noisy grading.

Appendix

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test

↖ Relates to: Slide 4 (Q1 Verdict)

ELI5

For each task, we have two scores: one for A and one for B. We look at the gap between them (A minus B).

If the new model is genuinely better, most gaps should be positive. This test checks whether the pattern of gaps is consistent enough to rule out random chance — without assuming scores follow a bell curve.

Think of it like: You flip a biased coin 2,699 times. The test asks: "Is this coin significantly biased toward heads, accounting for how much it flipped each direction?"

Technical details

Non-parametric alternative to the paired t-test. Appropriate when data are ordinal (Likert 1–5 scores) because the distance between scale points may not be uniform.

Procedure: Rank the absolute differences |scorecard_A − scorecard_B|, then check whether the sum of positive-difference ranks significantly exceeds what you'd expect under the null (no difference). Pairs where the score difference is exactly zero are excluded from the ranking — a standard part of the procedure that slightly reduces the effective sample size.

Result: p = 4.9e-69 — the probability of seeing a result this extreme if the models were equal is essentially zero. We reject the null: Model A is statistically significantly better.

Appendix

Mann-Kendall Trend Test

↖ Relates to: Slides 7–8 (Q2 Trend)

ELI5

Imagine 8 monthly sales reports. A monotonic trend means sales are generally moving in one direction overall — either upward or downward — even if there are a few flat months or minor bumps along the way. It does not require every single month to be strictly higher than the last.

This test looks at every possible pair of months and counts how often a later month is higher than an earlier one. If those "later is higher" pairs vastly outnumber the others, the test confirms a real trend.

Result here: τ = 0.50 (moderate positive tendency), but p = 0.11 — with only 8 data points, even a moderate correlation isn't statistically significant. The plateau is real.

Technical details

Kendall's τ measures rank correlation between batch order (1–8) and batch-level win rate. The Mann-Kendall test evaluates whether τ differs from zero under a permutation-based null distribution.

Why not linear regression? With only 8 points and no assumption that improvement is linear, a rank-based test is more appropriate and robust.

Result: τ = 0.500, p = 0.109. Moderate positive correlation but not significant at α = 0.05. We cannot reject the null of no monotonic trend across all 8 batches.

Note: low power (n=8 batches) limits sensitivity to real trends.

Krippendorff's Alpha (αK) — Inter-Rater Reliability

↖ Relates to: Slides 9–10 (Q3 Grader Quality)

ELI5

Imagine 5 film critics each rating the same movie. Even if they picked scores randomly, they'd sometimes agree by coincidence.

Krippendorff's alpha subtracts that random-agreement baseline before scoring consistency. A value of 0 means graders agree no more than chance; 1 means perfect agreement.

Our result: αK = 0.147 (poor agreement). But there's a critical caveat — see right.

Note: αK here is Krippendorff's reliability coefficient — not to be confused with the significance level α = 0.05 used elsewhere in this analysis.

Technical details + important caveat

Computed on the ordinal comparison scale using squared distance metric. Handles sparse data naturally (not all graders rate all prompts).

Benchmark: <0.20 poor · 0.20–0.40 fair · 0.40–0.60 moderate · >0.60 substantial.

Critical caveat: αK is computed across different batches where the underlying model changed. A grader rating prompt p100 in Batch 1 and a different grader rating p100 in Batch 6 may genuinely disagree — not because the graders are inconsistent, but because the model improved between batches. This conflates model improvement with grader inconsistency. True inter-rater reliability would require multiple graders rating the same model version.

Despite low αK, the overall finding (A wins 54%) is robust because it is based on 2,699 tasks.
